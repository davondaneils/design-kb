# Halden Hearing: strategy and brand brief

Baseline entry. Every claim below traces to the client brief unless marked "opinion" or "assumption".

## 1. The situation in one paragraph

Halden Hearing is two audiologists, Priya Nair and Tom Ashby, who left a national high-street chain in 2024 and opened one clinic in Clifton, Bristol: one clinic room, a soundproof booth, a small shop front. Their appointments run 90 minutes, they earn no commission, they fit aids from four manufacturers, and they also look after people who bought elsewhere. Their test is free with no obligation. The current site is a template with a stock couple laughing on a beach, which says nothing a chain could not also say. 40 percent of new patients come from a GP or a friend; most of the rest type "hearing test Bristol" into a search box.

## 2. Audience: a person and a moment, not a demographic

**Primary: the person who has finally decided to look into it.**
Picture someone in their late sixties in north Bristol. They have been turning the TV up and nodding along in restaurants for years (the brief: "most people wait about ten years"). Something tipped it: a grandchild mimicking them, a GP who said "you should get that checked", a friend who went somewhere and liked it. They are now on their phone or a laptop, often with a son or daughter beside them, searching "hearing test Bristol".

What they feel in that moment (assumption, to validate in interviews):
- Mild embarrassment, and a fear of being "sold to" before they have understood anything.
- A suspicion that a free test is a funnel into a 3,000 pound purchase.
- Low tolerance for small text, jargon and clutter.

What they need from the page in ten seconds: this is a real local clinic, the test is free and genuinely carries no obligation, it will not be rushed, and here is how to book or phone.

**Second reader: the adult child doing the research.** They compare tabs, look for prices and reviews, and forward a link. They need the facts laid out plainly so they can say "Mum, look at this one."

**Third: the existing hearing aid wearer who bought elsewhere** and is unhappy with fit, comfort or follow-up. They search for "hearing aid adjustment Bristol" or "earwax removal Bristol". The brief says Halden sees these people; most chains are built around their own sale, so this is an open door (opinion).

**The referrer.** 40 percent of new patients arrive by GP or friend. The site has to make the person who recommended Halden look good when their friend clicks through. That means the first screen must confirm what the friend said: unhurried, honest, local.

## 3. Positioning

Using April Dunford's structure (Obviously Awesome, 2019):

| Part | Halden |
|---|---|
| Competitive alternatives | Specsavers, Boots Hearingcare, Amplifon, two Bristol independents, and the biggest one: doing nothing for another few years. |
| Unique attributes (from the brief) | 90-minute appointments. No commission. Four manufacturers. Two named audiologists who used to work inside a chain. Sees people who bought elsewhere. Published price range including four years of aftercare. |
| Value to the patient | You understand your own hearing before anyone talks about a device. You can leave with answers and no purchase. If you do buy, the advice is not tied to a target. |
| Who cares most | People at the very start, who are wary of being sold to, and people let down by a quick sale elsewhere. |
| Market category | An independent audiology clinic. Not a "hearing centre" or a retailer. |

**Positioning statement (internal):**
For people in Bristol who have put off a hearing test for years and do not want to be sold a device on the day, Halden Hearing is an independent audiology clinic in Clifton where the first appointment is 90 minutes long, free and carries no obligation, and the audiologist earns no commission on anything you buy. It is run by two audiologists who left a high-street chain to work this way.

**Swap test.** Put "Specsavers" in that sentence and it breaks on three counts: the 90 minutes, "no commission", and "left a high-street chain". Put one of the other two independents in it and it breaks on the founders' story and, likely, on the 90 minutes (assumption: we have not verified their appointment lengths; check before launch, see handover).

**What we are deliberately not saying:**
- Not "cheapest". Halden publishes prices, but a price war with Specsavers is unwinnable for a one-room clinic (opinion).
- Not "latest technology". Every competitor can say it.
- Not anything negative about named competitors. The founders' story implies the contrast; the site never needs to name a chain. This keeps it legal, kind, and more credible (opinion).

## 4. The one true fact

**Your first appointment is 90 minutes, and nobody in the room is on commission.**

Everything else is a consequence of it: the test is unhurried, the results are explained, there is no decision on the day, the advice spans four makers. It is true (from the brief), it is concrete (a number you can hold them to), and it is the exact opposite of the moment the founders describe ("the chains sell them a device in one appointment").

The brand idea in four words: **Time, not a sale.**

## 5. Voice

Halden sounds like Priya or Tom across the desk: a clinician who explains things plainly and is in no hurry.

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Use numbers: 90 minutes, 65 pounds, four years. | Use "solutions", "cutting-edge", "life-changing". |
| Say "hearing loss" and "hearing aids" plainly. | Euphemise ("hearing journey", "better hearing experience"). |
| Write short sentences. One idea per sentence. | Stack clauses or use clinical jargon without explaining it. |
| Speak as "we" (Priya and Tom) to "you". | Write in the anonymous corporate third person. |
| Reassure with facts ("no obligation", "you can go home and think"). | Reassure with adjectives ("caring", "passionate", "trusted"). |
| Acknowledge the wait without blame. | Shame people for waiting or use fear ("dementia risk") as a hook. |

Readability target: reading age of about 9 to 11 (Flesch-Kincaid grade under 6 for key copy), which suits older readers and anyone reading on a phone. Source for plain-English practice: GOV.UK content design guidance (gov.uk/guidance/content-design/writing-for-gov-uk).

Sample lines:
- "You've waited long enough. We won't rush you."
- "You can leave with nothing but answers."
- "Already have hearing aids from somewhere else? Bring them in."

## 6. Visual identity decisions

Each decision has a reason that traces to the strategy. The brief asks for "trustworthy, not clinical, not like a chain".

### Type
- **Headlines: a book serif** (Charter, Iowan Old Style, Georgia fallback, all installed system fonts so no web font load). Reason: a serif reads as considered and personal, like a letter from a practice, which is the opposite of the clinical sans and bold retail type chains tend to use (opinion). Charter was designed for clarity at low resolution, so it holds up for older eyes.
- **Body: the system sans** (San Francisco, Segoe UI, Roboto). Reason: the most legible face each reader's device already renders well.
- **Sizes:** body 20px on desktop, 19px on mobile, never under 16px for anything a patient must read. Line height 1.55. Line length 60 to 70 characters. Reason: presbyopia is near-universal after 50 (source: American Academy of Ophthalmology, "Presbyopia"); the audience is mostly over 60. WCAG 2.2 1.4.4 also requires text to resize to 200 percent without loss, so all sizes are in rem.

### Color
- **Paper** `#F6F1E8`, a warm off-white, not a clinical white.
- **Ink** `#1E1A16`, a warm near-black (15.4:1 on paper).
- **Brick** `#8C3420`, the single accent, used for the primary button and key numbers (7.1:1 on paper, white on brick 8.0:1). Reason: Bristol is a red-brick and Pennant-stone city; a brick red is local and warm, and it sits outside the blues and greens the big chains use (opinion, based on their current sites). It also avoids the medical teal and hospital blue that read as clinical.
- **Dark mode:** background `#171411`, text `#EFE8DC` (15.1:1), accent lightened to `#EC9A7C` (8.3:1) so it keeps its warmth without vibrating.
- Every text pairing meets WCAG AAA (7:1) for body text. The audience justifies the stricter level.

### Imagery
- **Never:** stock couples, beaches, laughing, close-ups of a device in an ear on a white background. That is the current site and every chain's site.
- **Always:** the real place and the real people. Priya and Tom in the clinic room, natural light, eye contact. The booth door open. A chair, a desk, a printed audiogram being explained. Hands, not models.
- Reason: the brand's one fact is time and attention. Only photographs of the actual people and room can prove that; a stock photo silently contradicts it.
- Until the shoot happens, the page uses clearly labelled placeholders plus one graphic device (below), never stock.

### Graphic device: the 90-minute line
A thin horizontal rule marked in quarter-hours, used to show what happens across the appointment. It turns the one true fact into a visual the reader can scan. On a page it is a calm, typographic element, not an illustration.

### Mark direction
- **Wordmark:** "Halden" set in the serif, with "Hearing" in small caps sans beneath or beside. Reason: a named practice signs its name; a wordmark is the most honest form for two people and one room.
- **Symbol direction:** a circle with one open quarter. It reads as a sound arc and as a clock face with a quarter-hour of space in it: time made for you. Simple enough to cut in vinyl on the shop window and to emboss on a letter. Opinion, to be developed and tested with three alternatives (see handover).

### Layout
- One clear action per screen: "Book a free hearing test", with the phone number always visible as a real alternative, because many readers will prefer to call (assumption, test it).
- Generous space, one column of reading on mobile, a calm two-column hero on desktop. No carousels, no pop-ups, no chat widget on load. Reason: every interruption feels like a sales tactic, which is the exact thing the brand is against.
- Prices on the home page. Reason: the adult child researcher is looking for them, and publishing them is a trust act that matches "no commission".

## 7. Messaging hierarchy for the home page

1. Headline: the emotional truth (you have waited, we will not rush you).
2. Subhead: the one true fact as plain facts (90 minutes, free, no commission, no obligation).
3. Action: book or call.
4. Proof: two named audiologists, ex-chain, Clifton; four in ten patients come by GP or friend.
5. Section two: what happens in the 90 minutes, then prices, including for people who bought elsewhere.

# Research: Color psychology and brand

Accessed 2026-10-09. Status key as in 01.

## Key findings

| # | Claim | Source | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The color psychology literature is "at a nascent stage of development"; it is "premature to offer any bold theoretical statements, definitive empirical pronouncements". "Findings from small sampled studies should be considered preliminary." | Elliot, A. J. (2015). Color and psychological functioning. *Frontiers in Psychology* 6:368 | C (leading researcher's review) |
| 2 | Most theorizing covers one hue, red. Findings replicated across at least five labs (with some contrary studies): red gets an attention advantage; viewing red before a challenging cognitive task can undermine performance; red increases appraisals of aggressiveness and dominance; red in achievement contexts increases caution and avoidance. | Elliot (2015), Table 1 | X (replicated but with contrary studies, small samples) |
| 3 | "All color effects undoubtedly depend on certain conditions" such as culture, gender, age, task, and the exact color variant. | Elliot (2015) | C |
| 4 | The popular "62 to 90 percent of a snap judgment is based on color alone" figure appears in the abstract of Singh (2006), *Management Decision*, with no citation and no supporting data in the paper. | Singh (2006), "Impact of color on marketing" | C (the figure is uncited) |
| 5 | Labrecque and Milne (2012), "Exciting red and competent blue", four studies: hue maps onto brand personality dimensions, saturation and value modify them, and color can shift brand personality, purchase intent, likability and familiarity. The abstract does not give effect sizes. | *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* 40, 711 to 727 (abstract) | O (single paper set, lab context) |
| 6 | Red means danger in some cultures and is positive in others; Apple Stocks shows rising prices in green for English and red for Chinese. | Apple HIG, Color | C |
| 7 | Use color consistently; do not let one color mean two things. | Apple HIG, Color | C |

## What we found no good evidence for
- Specific hue-to-emotion tables ("blue = trust", "green = health", "orange = cheap") as causal effects on conversion. They are conventions within categories, not laws.
- "Red buttons convert better": we found only one-off A/B anecdotes. Our opinion (no source): any lift more likely comes from the button contrasting with the rest of the page than from the hue itself.

## Conflicting views
- Marketers and many design blogs present hue meanings as facts. Researchers (Elliot) urge caution. We side with the researchers and treat conventions as useful for category fit, not as psychology.

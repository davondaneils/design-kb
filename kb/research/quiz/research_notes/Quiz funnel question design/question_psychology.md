# Quiz funnel question design: the psychology and craft of personality-type lead-gen quiz questions

Scope: which question types and orders make people finish a personality-type lead-gen quiz, believe the result, and want the paid next step. House style: no em dashes; opinion is marked "(opinion)"; every claim carries an inline source.

## 1. Question order: easy, fun and identity first; sensitive or demographic later?

### Takeaway
Open with an easy, low-threat question that is clearly on topic, and save effortful or privacy-heavy items for later. The commitment evidence (foot-in-the-door) is real but small (r about .10), and the old rule "demographics always last" is weaker than people think: recent experiments find no breakoff penalty for putting simple demographics early. What actually drives breakoff is threat, effort and irrelevance, not position as such.

### Cited Findings
- Freedman and Fraser (1966), Experiment 2: 16.7% of homeowners contacted only once (N = 24) agreed to a large "Drive Carefully" lawn sign. Among those who had agreed two weeks earlier to a small request on the same issue in the same form (a small safe-driving sign), 76.0% agreed (N = 25). Even a different issue in a different form (a petition about keeping California beautiful) lifted compliance to about 47% ([Freedman and Fraser 1966, PDF](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Influence_Compliance/Freedman_Fraser_Foot-in-the-door.pdf); [Sage Encyclopedia of Social Psychology](https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/socialpsychology/chpt/footinthedoor-technique)).
- Across five meta-analyses (Beaman et al. 1983; Dillard et al. 1984; Fern et al. 1986; Burger 1999; Pascual and Gueguen 2005), the pooled foot-in-the-door effect is about r = .10, which is small ([Low-ball meta-analysis summarising FITD meta-analyses, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1162908816300470); [HAL preprint](https://hal.science/hal-03175652v1/document)).
- Burger (1999) puts the FITD effect down to several processes working together: self-perception, reactance, conformity, consistency, attributions and commitment. The same review explains why some FITD studies fail or even lower compliance ([Burger 1999, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15661679/)). A later paper citing Burger reports the technique works better when the first request takes more involvement and active decision-making ([Fennis et al., PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2935540/)).
- Cialdini rarely signs petitions, even for causes he supports, because of FITD (cited as an illustration of consistency pressure) ([iResearchNet, Encyclopedia of Social Psychology entry](https://psychology.iresearchnet.com/social-psychology/social-influence/foot-in-the-door-technique)).
- Krosnick and Presser's survey design handbook chapter recommends against placing difficult or sensitive items early, and notes this is partly why background and demographic items usually come at the end ([Krosnick and Presser 2010, Handbook of Survey Research](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2010/2010%20Handbook%20of%20Survey%20Research.pdf)).
- Duke's survey tipsheet: a sensitive question up front "raises a red flag" and makes breakoff more likely. Put sensitive items about halfway to two-thirds of the way through, after trust is built. Never open with a social-desirability item. Do not end on a sensitive item either. Ending on easy demographics is fine ([Duke DISM Tipsheet: Sensitive Questions](https://dism.duke.edu/files/2020/05/Tipsheet-Sensitive_Questions.pdf)). A federal ACF research brief gives the same middle-placement advice and adds "normalizing" lead-in language ([ACF RPG brief](https://rpg-cse.acf.gov/sites/default/files/2024-03/RPGBriefCollectingSensitiveInfo.pdf)).
- Tourangeau and Yan (2007) break sensitivity into three parts: intrusiveness, threat of disclosure and social desirability. Income is the most common item-nonresponse trigger ([ACF brief summarising Tourangeau and Yan 2007](https://rpg-cse.acf.gov/sites/default/files/2024-03/RPGBriefCollectingSensitiveInfo.pdf)).
- Harvard Shift Project randomised experiment (Facebook-recruited web surveys; demographics first, middle or last): "no effect of demographic item placement on survey attrition", and much lower item nonresponse when demographics came first ([Nguyen, Schneider, Harknett, Williams 2025](https://shift.hks.harvard.edu/dont-save-the-demographics-for-last/)). A Korean lab experiment likewise found no differences in response quality, social desirability bias or omission between demographics-first and demographics-last ([Cho and Byun 2012](https://www.kci.go.kr/kciportal/ci/sereArticleSearch/ciSereArtiView.kci?sereArticleSearchBean.artiId=ART001660486)).
- Survey Methods: Insights from the Field (2024): "some limited evidence" that putting salient, interesting topics first reduces breakoff. Topic interest and placement work together ([SMIF 2024](https://surveyinsights.org/?p=19071)).
- Web survey breakoff study: mobile respondents had 26% higher odds of breaking off, and each one-unit rise in item nonresponse raised breakoff odds by 3% ([Chen, Cernat, Shlomo, Eckman 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14707853211068008)).
- Levesque: a simple, non-threatening binary multiple-choice question before the email ask "counterintuitively, can actually increase your opt in rate" because it builds "action taking momentum" before the "big hairy scary question" of name and email ([Roger Dooley interview with Ryan Levesque](https://www.rogerdooley.com/ryan-levesque-choose)). He frames it as kaizen: small, non-threatening steps that "fly under the fight or flight radar" ([ASK Method Blueprint](https://s3.amazonaws.com/askmmc-19/AskMethodBlueprint.pdf); [Levesque on AWAI](https://www.awai.com/2018/07/8-micro-commitments-to-use-in-your-sales-process)).
- Practitioner guidance (CRO): lead with the most accessible question, grounded in a positive, recent experience rather than an abstract idea; show the total number of steps from the first screen; add a mid-quiz progress reward ([GrowthLens 2026](https://www.growthlens.io/blog/quiz-funnel-completion-rate-optimization)). This is practitioner opinion, not controlled evidence.

### Inferences
- Rule: Q1 should be easy, fast, plainly linked to the promised result, and identity-flavoured ("Which of these sounds most like your weekend?"). Do not open with a demographic or a struggle question. The FITD effect is small, so the first question's main job is to cut perceived effort and threat, not to "lock in" commitment.
- Rule: put the most self-revealing item (biggest obstacle, frustration) at about the 50 to 70% point, then end on light, easy items before the email gate. Ending on an easy note keeps momentum into the opt-in.
- Rule: simple, non-threatening demographics (age band, training experience) can go early with no proven breakoff cost, especially if they visibly personalise the next questions. Income or anything intrusive still belongs late, or nowhere.
- Expert move (inference): FITD needs the first act to be self-attributed. Burger's self-perception route means Q1 should feel freely chosen and slightly expressive ("I'm someone who..."), not a mindless click. Never reward or bribe the first answer.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found that tests question order inside marketing quizzes specifically. The evidence comes from social-research surveys and compliance experiments.
- Burger's (1999) specific moderator effect sizes (delay, same vs different requester, prosocial requests) were not retrieved in full text.

## 2. Question types: scenario/SJT, image choice, self-description, Likert, forced choice

### Takeaway
For a short marketing quiz, single-choice items with 3 to 5 concrete, equally attractive options (scenario or "which sounds like you") are the best fit. Forced choice between options balanced for desirability resists faking and the pull toward a flattering "ideal" answer, but it can feel harder. Long Likert grids lower satisfaction. Pictures help when they are the answer choices people tap, and hurt when people must produce an image themselves.

### Cited Findings
- Cao and Drasgow (2019) meta-analysis: forced-choice personality measures show an overall score-inflation effect of only 0.06 between low and high stakes, much lower than single-statement (Likert) measures for most facets. FC works best when the statements in a block are balanced for social desirability ([Cao and Drasgow 2019, APA PsycNet](https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/apl0000414)).
- Martinez and Salgado (2021) meta-analysis: FC inventories resist faking. Quasi-ipsative FC resists best (conscientiousness inflation d = 0.49 vs 1.27 for fully ipsative FC). Faking is larger in lab experiments than in real applicant samples ([Martinez and Salgado 2021, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34659043)).
- Heggestad et al. (2006): under faking instructions, a multidimensional FC measure showed less group-level score inflation than Likert measures ([APA PsycNet](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2006-00819-003)).
- Huber, Kuncel et al. (2021): MFC scales "substantially reduced motivated score elevation", but people still faked selectively on dimensions that looked relevant to the job ([Personnel Assessment and Decisions](https://scholarworks.bgsu.edu/pad/vol7/iss1/3)).
- Respondent experience: the FC format affected self-reported test-taking ease, anxiety and positive affect, and applicants may react less positively to FC than to single-statement inventories (Converse et al. 2008, as cited in [Salgado 2015 meta-analysis, JOOP](https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/joop.12098)). One TIRT development paper reports FC tests usually take less time because they are shorter ([PMC 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11673971)).
- Situational judgment tests present scenarios and ask respondents to judge candidate actions. They are a kind of job simulation ([McDaniel and Whetzel 2007, APA PsycNet](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2007-07684-009)).
- Grids vs item-by-item: grids are faster, but respondents' satisfaction with the survey drops (Thorndike et al. 2009; Toepoel et al. 2009, cited in [Revilla et al. 2017, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0736585316300776)).
- Answering open questions by supplying images (photo upload) raised noncompliance and completion time and made the survey experience worse (N = 3,043 RCT) ([Bosch, Revilla et al. 2022, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36247522)). That is a different thing from tapping an image as an answer option.
- Pictures in web surveys change how people read a question (visual context effects) ([Couper, Tourangeau and Kenyon 2004, Public Opinion Quarterly](https://surveyinsights.org/?p=4350&print=pdf)). In a choice experiment, adding images made people weight visually salient attributes more ([Shr et al. 2019, Ecological Economics](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0921800918301009)).
- Satisficing: faster, lower-skill web respondents show primacy effects (picking the first acceptable option) in visually presented lists ([Malhotra 2008, POQ](https://academic.oup.com/poq/article/72/5/914/1832496)).
- Levesque: keep each question to "between 3 and 5" answer options, because 17 options "slow people" and give "a reason to bail"; also avoid questions that are "too personal, too soon" or hard to think through ([Veterinary Marketing Podcast ep. 272](https://www.veterinarymarketingpodcast.com/episode/vmp-272-ryan-levesque-shares-how-to-use-quiz-funnels-for-your-veterinary-practice)). His quiz blueprint lists the formats he uses: radio multiple choice, checkbox multiple choice, short input and long input ([ASK Quiz Funnel Blueprint](https://quiz-funnel-masterclass.s3.amazonaws.com/ASK_QuizFunnel_Blueprint.pdf)).
- Okendo (quiz vendor) recommends 5 to 10 questions, simple language, and images on answer options so takers choose "more confidently" ([Okendo Help Center](https://support.okendo.io/en/articles/9077941-quizzes-completion-rate-optimization)). This is vendor opinion.

### Inferences
- Rule: default format is one tap, single choice, 3 to 5 options, written as concrete scenarios or self-descriptions ("After a hard session you usually..."). This is effectively a light, quasi-ipsative forced choice: each option maps to a type, and every option is equally flattering, which the FC faking literature shows is the lever that stops everyone picking the "ideal" answer.
- Rule: avoid Likert batteries and grids in a lead-gen quiz. They lower satisfaction, invite straight-lining, and give a weaker "it gets me" feeling, because a 1 to 5 agree scale does not make a person choose who they are.
- Rule: use images as answer tiles only where the visual itself carries meaning (style, setting, mood). Never ask the user to upload or create something.
- Rule: randomise or rotate option order where it does not hurt the narrative, to blunt primacy from fast mobile tappers.
- Expert move: balance options for desirability. If one option is plainly "the good answer" (for example "I never skip training"), most people pick it, type distribution collapses, and buckets merge (see Levesque's "collapsed buckets" warning in section 3).

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found that compares scenario vs self-description vs image-tile items for perceived accuracy of marketing quiz results.
- Response-time data per item type in consumer quizzes was not found. Vendor completion benchmarks exist (section 3) but are not broken down by item type.

## 3. Ryan Levesque's Ask Method: deep-dive survey, segmentation, buckets, micro-commitments, stated numbers

### Takeaway
Levesque's system runs in two stages. First, a one-off open-ended Deep Dive Survey finds the market's 3 to 5 "buckets" and its language. Second, a short micro-commitment quiz (3 to 5 answer options per question, easy questions first, one or more segmentation questions) puts each person in a bucket. A diagnosis-style result then "prescribes" a tailored offer. His numbers are self-reported and mostly anecdotal.

### Cited Findings
- Four surveys: Deep Dive, Micro-Commitment Bucket, "Do You Hate Me", and Pivot. Six steps: Prepare, Persuade, Segment, Prescribe, Profit, Pivot ([Cooler Insights summary of Ask](https://coolerinsights.com/2023/11/effective-sales-funnel-ask-method); [Matthew Mottola notes on Ask, PDF](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/56b8be1227d4bda9ae42b4ca/t/579a9b602e69cf766304bffa/1469750113717/Ryan+Levesque,+ASK.pdf)).
- Deep Dive Survey: open-ended, run once per market. It finds what people "secretly want" and which buckets exist (for example, fitness splits into lose weight / get huge / tone). The core question is the SMIQ (Single Most Important Question): "What is your single biggest challenge, or struggle, related to XYZ?" ([Mottola notes](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/56b8be1227d4bda9ae42b4ca/t/579a9b602e69cf766304bffa/1469750113717/Ryan+Levesque,+ASK.pdf)). Levesque's logic: "people don't always know what they want. But people do know what they don't want" ([Same Side Selling podcast 071](https://samesidesellingacademy.com/podcast/071-ryan-levesque-asking-the-right-questions)).
- Analysis rule: weight the longest open-ended answers (top 20%) as "hyper-responsives" and echo their exact language in copy ([Mottola notes](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/56b8be1227d4bda9ae42b4ca/t/579a9b602e69cf766304bffa/1469750113717/Ryan+Levesque,+ASK.pdf); [SoBrief summary of Ask](https://sobrief.com/books/ask)). One one-to-one conversation "is worth 25 survey responses" ([Making Bank S2E37](https://joshfelber.com/makingbank/single-important-question-guest-ryan-levesque-makingbank-s2e37)).
- Buckets: "Identify your 3-5 most important groups of people in your market." The four ways to bucket are Journey, Challenge, Situation and Hybrid. He also distinguishes "Pure vs. Perceived Customization", "Primary vs. Secondary Segmentation" and "Multi-Question Outcome Mapping" ([ASK Method Blueprint](https://s3.amazonaws.com/askmmc-19/AskMethodBlueprint.pdf)). Pure customization means different diagnoses lead to different offers ([Quiz Funnel Blueprint](https://quiz-funnel-masterclass.s3.amazonaws.com/ASK_QuizFunnel_Blueprint.pdf)).
- Micro-Commitment Bucket Survey: open with a casual multiple-choice question before more private ones, ask only questions that refine the bucket, then pitch ([Four Minute Books summary](https://fourminutebooks.com/ask-summary)). Per one summary of Ask: easy opening questions, then context for personalisation, then a closing segmentation question that identifies the main challenge, with a manageable number of choices and an option for people who do not fit ([SoBrief](https://sobrief.com/books/ask)).
- Three quiz frameworks: Score, Type, Killer. Type draws on "Who am I?" self-discovery. Killer draws on loss aversion ("fear of LOSS is GREATER than the promise of GAIN", attributed to Kahneman). Score draws on social comparison and need for achievement ([Quiz Funnel Blueprint](https://quiz-funnel-masterclass.s3.amazonaws.com/ASK_QuizFunnel_Blueprint.pdf)).
- Questions as content: "Your Questions are CONTENT", "Must create demand for your offer", "Questions already help people prepare for the offer", "Break down old belief and build up new one". Results give the "Band-aid" (what people think they need) and open a loop to the "Cure" (what they really need, and what you sell) ([Quiz Funnel Blueprint](https://quiz-funnel-masterclass.s3.amazonaws.com/ASK_QuizFunnel_Blueprint.pdf)). Diagnosis "creates curiosity, desire and demand for your prescription" and "The right questions build your authority as the trusted expert" (same source).
- Stated numbers (all self-reported, marketing material):
  - Answer options: 3 to 5 per question ([VMP ep. 272](https://www.veterinarymarketingpodcast.com/episode/vmp-272-ryan-levesque-shares-how-to-use-quiz-funnels-for-your-veterinary-practice)).
  - Buckets: 3 to 5 ([ASK Method Blueprint](https://s3.amazonaws.com/askmmc-19/AskMethodBlueprint.pdf)).
  - Segmentation "can often 2-3X your conversion rates"; one case cites a "61% conversion rate" ([Quiz Funnel Blueprint](https://quiz-funnel-masterclass.s3.amazonaws.com/ASK_QuizFunnel_Blueprint.pdf)).
  - Individual quizzes "generate as much as 5,000 to 25,000 email subscribers per day" ([ASK Method Blueprint](https://s3.amazonaws.com/askmmc-19/AskMethodBlueprint.pdf)). One of his own quizzes adds "50,000 emails ... every day" ([Hustle and Flowchart](https://hustleandflowchart.com/ryan-levesque-list-growth-funnels)).
  - Bucket.io reports "82+ million Quiz Takers" ([askmethod.com](https://askmethod.com/why-ask-method)).
  - The company itself now says the 2014 book version is "no longer best practice" ([askmethod.com](https://askmethod.com/about)).
- The pre-opt-in segmentation question raised conversion in a split test Levesque shared, according to a practitioner who heard it in a mastermind. The same practitioner warns that a lopsided bucket split signals "collapsed" buckets ([James Wedmore blog](https://www.jameswedmoretraining.com/blog/how-i-doubled-sales-with-the-ask-method)). This is second-hand and anecdotal.
- Optimal question count: the Quiz Funnel Masterclass promises to teach "the optimal number of questions, the optimal question length, and the optimal number of answers", but the number itself is behind the paywall ([Web Market Support review](https://webmarketsupport.com/quiz-funnel-masterclass-review)).
- Vendor benchmarks for context: Interact reports an average 65% start-to-finish and 40.1% start-to-lead across its portfolio ([Interact 2026 report](https://www.tryinteract.com/blog/quiz-conversion-rate-report)). Outgrow reports 65 to 85% completion for 3 to 7 questions, 45 to 65% for 8 to 15, and 25 to 45% for 16+ ([Outgrow](https://outgrow.co/blog/quiz-engagement-benchmarks-completion-rates)). Typeform found forms with fewer than six questions and under one minute had higher completion ([Typeform](https://www.typeform.com/blog/how-to-leverage-forms-at-every-stage-of-the-marketing-funnel)). All are vendor data with undisclosed methods.

### Inferences
- Rule: for a personality-type quiz selling a paid next step, build about 6 to 9 questions. Most should be type-scoring identity items. One or two should be segmentation items (goal or obstacle) that decide which offer angle the result shows. One optional easy demographic item can go early. (Inference from the vendor completion curves plus Levesque's structure, not from a Levesque number.)
- Rule: segmentation buckets (offer angle) and personality types (result label) are different axes. Levesque's "Primary vs. Secondary Segmentation" and "Multi-Question Outcome Mapping" support keeping them separate: the type flatters and verifies, and the bucket routes the offer.
- Rule: write the questions from Deep Dive language. If there is no deep dive yet, mine reviews, comments and DMs for the exact phrases (Interact's founder gives the same advice: [Interact](https://www.tryinteract.com/blog/quiz-conversion-rate-report)).
- Watch-out: a skewed distribution (for example 70% in one bucket) means the options are not balanced or two buckets have merged. Fix the wording before scaling.

### Gaps
- Levesque's stated optimal question count was not found in public sources. It sits inside the paid Masterclass.
- No independent verification of the "2-3X" or "61%" claims.
- The full text of Ask (2015) was not retrieved, so the book's exact Micro-Commitment Bucket Survey example question count is unconfirmed. One summary lists a B2B example that starts "Full-time or part-time; Business type/niche; ..." but it is truncated ([Cooler Insights](https://coolerinsights.com/2023/11/effective-sales-funnel-ask-method)).

## 4. Making results feel accurate: Barnum/Forer, self-verification, why 16Personalities/Enneagram/BuzzFeed feel right

### Takeaway
People rate generic descriptions as highly accurate when three things hold: the description is favourable, they believe it came from their own answers, and the source looks credible. On top of that, people seek and trust feedback that confirms how they already see themselves. A result feels right when it mirrors the person's answers back, flatters with a two-sided trait, and names something they already believe about themselves.

### Cited Findings
- Forer (1949): 39 students took his Diagnostic Interest Blank. A week later each got the same 13-statement sketch, assembled from a newsstand astrology book, presented as personal. The mean accuracy rating was 4.26 on a 0 to 5 scale ([Psychology.com on Forer](https://psychology.com/biases/forer-effect); [Atticus Li on Forer](https://atticusli.com/replication-crisis/forer-barnum-effect)). Secondary sources often quote 4.30. The primary-paper figure is 4.26 ([Atticus Li](https://atticusli.com/replication-crisis/forer-barnum-effect); [Wikipedia gives 4.30](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnum_effect)). Forer's abstract: "Minimum correspondence with self-evaluation seems to encourage acceptance of a total diagnosis, although specific statements are evaluated more cautiously" ([APA PsycNet](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1949-03749-001)).
- Snyder, Shenkel and Lowery (1977) review: acceptance depends mainly on situational factors rather than on the type of person. Feedback presented as specifically for the individual, and from a source with diagnostic authority, is accepted more ([APA PsycNet](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1977-21271-001); summarised in [Atticus Li](https://atticusli.com/replication-crisis/forer-barnum-effect)).
- Dickson and Kelly (1985) review: acceptance rises with favourable content, with the belief that the assessment came from one's own answers, and with the deliverer's status. Statements that cover both ends of a trait ("sometimes outgoing, sometimes reserved") are accepted by both outgoing and reserved readers ([Psychology.com summarising Dickson and Kelly](https://psychology.com/biases/forer-effect); [Sage](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2466/pr0.1985.57.2.367)).
- Having actually taken a test raises acceptance of bogus statements (Jackson 1978, "The effects of test taking on acceptance of bogus personality statements"; listed in [Dickson and Kelly references](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2466/pr0.1985.57.2.367)). Source status and message favourability both raise acceptance (Halperin et al. 1976, in [Furnham and Schofield 1987 review](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf02686623)). People most readily accept positive statements, especially when some negatives are offset by positives ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/science/Barnum-Effect)).
- Counterpoint: Layne (1979) argues people recognise the triviality of Barnum feedback, and that bogus feedback only slightly shifts self-evaluations ([Layne 1979, Ovid](https://www.ovid.com/journals/jccpy/pdf/00004730-197902000-00038~the-barnum-effect-rationality-versus-gullibility)).
- Self-verification (Swann): people prefer others to see them as they see themselves, even when the self-view is negative. Self-verifying feedback makes the world feel "coherent and predictable" and lowers anxiety ([Swann 2012, Handbook of Theories of Social Psychology](https://sk.sagepub.com/hnbk/edvol/hdbk_socialpsychtheories2/chpt/selfverification-theory)). People judge feedback that matches crystallised self-perceptions as accurate ([Learning and Instruction 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959475222001360)). Swann, Pelham and Krull (1989): overall, participants leaned toward favourable feedback, but sought confirming feedback on attributes they were certain of ([Simply Psychology](https://www.simplypsychology.org/self-verification-theory.html)). Among married couples, self-verification beat self-enhancement in a meta-analysis (Kwang and Swann 2010) ([Swann chapter PDF](https://labs.la.utexas.edu/swann/files/2016/02/svt-lange-et-al.pdf)).
- BuzzFeed's quiz lead (2014): "our most successful quizzes are mostly built so that the results feel personal and that you can relate to them. The answers genuinely correspond to the results". The team writes the results first, then the answers ([HuffPost interview](https://www.huffpost.com/entry/buzzfeed-quiz-how-do-they-work_n_4810992)).
- Personality quizzes satisfy curiosity about oneself and give social validation when results that confirm existing self-beliefs are shared (psychologist quoted in [Business Insider 2019](https://www.businessinsider.com/why-we-like-personality-quizzes-2019-2)). "Feeling understood and normal is a powerful experience" (Fayard, Psychology Today, as quoted in [CUNY student essay](https://subureng110.commons.gc.cuny.edu/2020/05/19/do-personality-tests-help-in-identity)). That is second-hand; the original Psychology Today piece was not fetched.
- Risk: for people sensitive to constraint, even a positive or neutral self-knowledge result can feel limiting ("being one thing precludes them from being another") ([JSTOR Daily, citing research](https://daily.jstor.org/what-do-personality-quizzes-really-tell-you)).
- Levesque names "Self-Discovery", "Everyone's favorite subject: Me, Myself, and I" and "Curiosity" as quiz psychological triggers ([ASK Method Blueprint](https://s3.amazonaws.com/askmmc-19/AskMethodBlueprint.pdf)).
- Identity labels change behaviour: in Bryan, Walton, Rogers and Dweck (2011), wording a survey about "being a voter" (noun) rather than "voting" (verb) raised validated turnout by 10.9 percentage points in one experiment ([PNAS 2011, PDF](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/59faaf5b01b9500001e95457/5bc55edda8a9d806acce2dbd_Bryan%2C%20C.%20J.%2C%20Walton%2C%20G.%20M.%2C%20Rogers%2C%20T.%2C%20%26%20Dweck%2C%20C.%20S.%202011.pdf)). An explicit label makes a consumer's identity more predictive of behaviour (Tybout and Yalch 1980, cited in [Forehand, Deshpande, Reed 2002, J Applied Psychology](https://www.proquest.com/docview/614375899/fulltextPDF)).

### Inferences
- Rule: the result page must visibly use the person's own answers ("Because you picked X and Y..."). This is the "derived from my answers" lever, which costs nothing and raises acceptance.
- Rule: write each type as a strength with an honest, two-sided shadow ("You push hard, which is why rest feels like losing"). Favourable plus a small, credible negative is accepted more readily than pure flattery, and it reads as insight rather than horoscope.
- Rule: give the type an identity noun ("You're a Grinder", not "You tend to grind"). The noun-label evidence suggests it strengthens both belief and follow-through.
- Rule: "every answer is a good answer". Each option should lead to a type the person would be happy to be. That combines self-verification (they picked it) with self-enhancement (it is positive).
- Ethics line (opinion): using Barnum mechanics to make true, answer-driven content feel personal is fine. Using them to dress up a result that ignores the answers is deceptive and fragile. If people retake the quiz or compare results with a friend, the trick is exposed.
- Expert move (inference): a result that is too narrow can trigger the "constraint" reaction. Add a secondary trait or "your edge to develop" so the label opens doors rather than closing them. That edge is also the natural bridge to the paid step.

### Gaps
- No published data was found on 16Personalities' or the Enneagram Institute's own accuracy ratings or design rationale.
- No study was found that measures Barnum acceptance specifically in online marketing quizzes.

## 5. Pre-selling through questions without feeling salesy; risks of pain questions for a reward brand

### Takeaway
Simply asking people about a goal or an intended behaviour nudges them toward it (the question-behaviour effect: small, d about 0.1 to 0.3, strongest for desirable, easy behaviours). A goal or desire question ("What do you most want to get stronger at?") therefore pre-sells without pressure. Gain framing is at least as persuasive as loss framing for promotion-type goals, produces less counter-arguing, and matches a brand that sells reward. Pain questions work for diagnosis, but they should be optional in tone and turned into a positive at the result.

### Cited Findings
- Wood et al. (2016) meta-analysis of 116 tests (N = 54,985): asking intention or self-prediction questions has a small positive effect on later behaviour (d+ = 0.24). The effect is larger for socially desirable, less risky and easy-to-do behaviours ([Wood et al. 2016, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4931712/); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26162771)).
- Spangenberg et al. (2016), 104 studies: r+ = .137, with support for attitude, consistency, fluency and motivation mechanisms ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1057740815001102)). Wilding et al. (2016): g = 0.14, and prediction questions show the largest effect ([Tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10463283.2016.1245940)). Measuring purchase intentions has been found to increase future purchases (Chandon, Morwitz and Reinartz 2004, cited in the same source).
- Caution: Rodrigues et al. (2015) found a smaller health-behaviour effect (SMD = 0.09), with publication bias, and say the effect "may be overestimated" ([APA PsycNet](https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/hea0000104)). The Wood review found no effect on risky behaviours (d = -0.05) ([Frontiers commentary](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00879/full)).
- Framing: across 93 studies (N = 21,656), gain-framed prevention messages were slightly more persuasive than loss-framed ones (r = .03) ([O'Keefe and Jensen 2007, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17934940)). For detection (finding a problem), loss framing was slightly better (r = -.04) ([O'Keefe and Jensen 2009](https://academic.oup.com/joc/article-abstract/59/2/296/4098529)). For actual prevention behaviour, gain framing won (r = 0.083), especially for physical activity ([Gallagher and Updegraff 2012, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21993844/)).
- Gain-framed narratives produce less counter-arguing (d = -0.57), more positive message evaluation (d = 0.25) and more self-efficacy (d = 0.17) than loss-framed ones (47 studies, N = 16,361) ([Kim and Chen 2025, Journal of Communication](https://academic.oup.com/joc/article-abstract/75/3/220/8011622)).
- Regulatory fit: gain frames persuade more when the message is promotion-focused, and loss frames when it is prevention-focused, partly through processing fluency ("feeling right") ([Lee and Aaker 2004, JPSP](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2004-10747-001)).
- Levesque's counter-position: the "Killer" quiz framework relies on loss aversion, and the SMIQ asks about "biggest challenge, frustration, obstacle, or hurdle" ([Quiz Funnel Blueprint](https://quiz-funnel-masterclass.s3.amazonaws.com/ASK_QuizFunnel_Blueprint.pdf); [Same Side Selling 071](https://samesidesellingacademy.com/podcast/071-ryan-levesque-asking-the-right-questions)). He also lists "Desire" as a segmentation route and says diagnosis "Show[s] empathy and understanding" ([ASK Method Blueprint](https://s3.amazonaws.com/askmmc-19/AskMethodBlueprint.pdf); [Quiz Funnel Blueprint](https://quiz-funnel-masterclass.s3.amazonaws.com/ASK_QuizFunnel_Blueprint.pdf)).
- Ending a survey on a sensitive or unpleasant item risks leaving respondents "suspicious or offended" ([Duke tipsheet](https://dism.duke.edu/files/2020/05/Tipsheet-Sensitive_Questions.pdf)).
- Levesque: a quiz "will ... feel like a helpful recommendation" when it diagnoses and then prescribes ([Fyrebox quoting Levesque](https://www.fyrebox.com/blog/how-turn-quiz-complete-sales-funnel)). This is a secondary quote with no primary source.
- Practitioner advice: frame the email gate as delivery ("Where should we send your results?") rather than a toll, and preview the type before the gate ([GrowthLens](https://www.growthlens.io/blog/quiz-funnel-completion-rate-optimization)). This is opinion, untested in public.

### Inferences
- Rule: for a reward-led brand, ask the desire question in gain form ("What do you want to get stronger at?", "What would a great month of training look like?"). Its answer is the offer's hook. The question-behaviour effect is largest for desirable, easy behaviours, so a light goal question works better than a heavy pain probe.
- Rule: ask about the obstacle once, mid-quiz, worded as a neutral "what gets in the way" with answers that are normal and blameless ("Time", "Not sure what to do next", "Motivation dips"). Normalising wording is the survey literature's fix for sensitivity ([ACF brief](https://rpg-cse.acf.gov/sites/default/files/2024-03/RPGBriefCollectingSensitiveInfo.pdf)). At the result, recast the obstacle as the next unlock, so the paid step is the prescribed "cure" in gain terms.
- Rule: one self-prediction question near the end ("How likely are you to train 3x a week next month?") is the strongest form of the question-behaviour effect. Use it sparingly, and only where a yes is easy and desirable. (Inference; the effect size is small and contested.)
- Risk (opinion): a quiz that stacks pain questions turns a reward brand into a deficit brand. Loss-framed questions suit "detection" offers (finding a problem); for "promotion" offers they only hurt fit and invite counter-arguing.
- Expert move (inference): make questions teach. Levesque's "Your Questions are CONTENT". An answer option can quietly plant the brand's belief (for example, an option that names progressive overload or recovery as the lever), so the result's prescription feels already agreed. Keep it honest. If an option exists only to sell, people notice.

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found comparing desire-led vs pain-led quiz questions on paid conversion.
- No public data was found on how many pain vs goal questions top quiz funnels use. Levesque's ratio, if he has one, is not public.

# Research 05: Strategy to visual identity

Researcher, 2026-10-13. Brief: `kb/briefs/strategy/05-strategy-to-visual-identity.md`. Revised 2026-10-13 after the critic's spot check (`kb/reviews/design-strategy.md`): E7 attribution corrected, E13 quote corrected, E2 checked against the full text, quadrant names aligned with Romaniuk 2018, rows E16 to E30 added for the v0.1.1 gaps (tracking, portfolio cohesion, semiotic codes, brand behaviors).

## Key findings
1. **Distinctive assets are measured on fame and uniqueness.** Ehrenberg-Bass: distinctive assets "are non-brand name elements, such as colours, logos or characters that uniquely signal the brand" [E1]. The Ehrenberg-Bass web page labels the quadrants Use or Lose, Invest, Avoid and Test or ignore [E1]. Romaniuk's book (2018), as cited in the 2026 benchmark, names them **Use or Lose** (high fame, high uniqueness), **Investment Potential** (low fame, high uniqueness), **Avoid Solo Use** (high fame, low uniqueness) and **Ignore or Test** (low, low), with a 50 percent threshold on each axis [E2h]. The skill uses the book's names. Avoid quadrant: "such elements will trigger competitors to copy" [E1b]. "All new assets start with low uniqueness and fame." [E1]
2. **Benchmark (Phua, Bali, Anesbury and Sharp, International Journal of Advertising, 2026; 1,162 assets from 128 brands, 21 categories, 4 countries, 9 years, 12,249 respondents, all category buyers, 229 to 1,500 per questionnaire) [E2][E2b][E2g]:** shape-based assets (logos, packaging) average 40 percent fame and 71 percent uniqueness; color assets 12 percent fame and 39 percent uniqueness, the weakest type [E2]. The average asset: 26 percent fame, 54 percent uniqueness; quadrants split at 50 percent [E2]. "Reliance on color alone as a Distinctive Asset, therefore, carries substantial risk." [E2] Color also has the highest competitive intensity (HHI 0.31); characters, logos and fonts the lowest (0.69, 0.61, 0.60) [E2, citing Ward et al. 2020]. Strong case: the GEICO Gecko, 87 percent fame and 98 percent uniqueness (Romaniuk 2018, cited in E2). All four of these were confirmed in the full text on 2026-10-13 (the HTML full text is reachable through a fetch service; tandfonline blocks direct requests) [E2c][E2d]. Limits stated by the authors: the data are "a cross-sectional snapshot rather than a longitudinal perspective", and only assets still in use were measured [E2i]. Fame and uniqueness correlate strongly across assets (r = 0.97) [E2i]. Services exception: "narrative-driven assets perform relatively better in service industries"; in services "narrative-driven assets such as stories may serve as efficient branding tools" [E2f].
3. **Test assets by recognition, not liking.** Caruso et al. (2025), 227 marketers on their last pack redesign: 195 (86 percent) involved research, and there "was no evidence that marketers who conducted market research had more overall success"; focus groups and brand attitudes "are associated with less successful outcomes", while testing that identifies elements consumers link with the brand is "linked to more successful pack redesigns" [E16]. The 2026 paper relays it: "packaging redesigns based on focus groups and brand attitude measures has been associated with less successful outcomes, whereas approaches grounded in Distinctive Asset research are linked to more successful outcomes (Caruso et al. 2025)" [E2].
4. **Attention-grabbing creative needs stronger branding.** Romaniuk: "The more attention-grabbing the creative, the harder the branding has to work to succeed" [E3].
5. **Shape carries attributes, weakly, and words override it.** Jiang, Gorn, Galli and Chattopadhyay (2016, Journal of Consumer Research 42(5)), five experiments: "circular- versus angular-logo shapes activate softness and hardness associations", which shift judgments such as a shoe's comfort or durability; but "There are no logo shape effects on attribute judgments ... when the headline of the ad highlights a product attribute that differs from the inference drawn from the logo shape" [E4].
6. **Sound and shape map across cultures.** Ćwiek et al. (2022, Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B): bouba/kiki tested in 25 languages, 9 language families, 10 writing systems; "strong evidence for the effect across languages" [E5]. Supports rounded or angular letterforms and marks as a cross-cultural cue (R, our inference for brand use).
7. **Typeface persona is perceived** (Brumberger 2003) [D5 in research 04].
8. **Asset equity is real and can be lost.** Tropicana 2009: PepsiCo scrapped a January carton redesign within weeks and brought back the old pack and "the longtime Tropicana brand symbol, an orange from which a straw protrudes" (NYT) [E6]. The sales figures are Information Resources Inc. (IRI) data reported by Ad Age and relayed by BusinessWeek on 2009-04-02: unit sales down 20 percent and dollar sales down 19 percent, about $33 million, to $137 million, between January 1 and February 22 [E7]. Several secondary sources say "Nielsen"; that attribution is wrong. The Ad Age original (article_id=135735) is blocked from here.
9. **Earned fame permits simplification.** Mastercard 2019 dropped its name from the symbol "with more than 80 percent of people spontaneously recognizing the Mastercard Symbol without the word 'mastercard'" [E8].
10. **Accidental assets count.** Monzo's coral card "was more of a happy accident than a marketing masterstroke" (WIRED) [E9]; the 2022 refresh "audited our visual brand codes" and kept coral as "what helps keep us distinctive"; it broke category imagery codes on purpose: "The banking market is flooded with generic people photography. So we needed a style that was unique to Monzo." [E10]
11. **A treatment can be the asset**: COLLINS' Spotify duotone (Design Week, via art direction research C7) [E11].
12. **Why distinctive, emotional brand work matters (budget context).** Binet and Field, IPA databank (996 campaigns, 700 brands, 83 categories [E12]): chart titles "The 60:40 split delivers maximum effectiveness" and "Emotional campaigns yield stronger long-term business effects" (The Long and the Short of It, figures 38, 44, 53, 54; Tom Roach's compilation, extracted with `pdftotext -raw` on 2026-10-13) [E13]. For B2B (2019, LinkedIn B2B Institute) "Activation ... should get 54% of spend while brand building should receive 46%" (The Drum) [E14]. These are databank averages, not laws (kb/references/strategy-brand.md).
13. **Color evidence is weak and conventions are not causes** (design-color psychology-brand: Elliot 2015; Labrecque and Milne 2012 as "a weak nudge") [E15].
14. **Choosing an asset for its meaning costs uniqueness.** Romaniuk (Marketing Week, 2025) names four life stages of an asset (selection, building, use, protection) and warns that meaning-led selection leads to "Reduced distinctiveness" because brands drawing on the same source pick the same asset; "It's not about picking a winner, it's about picking a contender." Building is "widespread co-presentation of the asset with the brand name" [E17]. Bali et al. (2024) found suggestive assets show "lower levels of Fame and Uniqueness than non-suggestive Distinctive Assets" [E18]. Tension with the attribute table (opinion): attribute fit may choose among contenders, but uniqueness against the wall decides.
15. **Tracking after launch.** Romaniuk's book has a chapter section "The right times to measure" and, per the publisher, sets up "a Distinctive Asset management system to provide an early warning system" (table of contents [E19]; description [E19b]; a tier 5 summary relays more [E20]). The 2026 benchmark calls fame and uniqueness benchmarks "a practical tool for prioritizing Distinctive Assets" [E2h]. Dawes gives the in-market arithmetic that sets a sensible cadence: with a two-year interpurchase time, "50% are in the market over the course of a (whole) year, or around 13% in any quarter" [E21]. Cadence rules in the skill are opinion built on these.
16. **Portfolio cohesion is measurable without a survey.** Ward, Trinh, Beal, Dawes and Romaniuk (2025): products from high-cohesion portfolios had 17 percent higher correct brand recall and were matched to their portfolio by 79 versus 35 percent of respondents; "the best way for a brand portfolio to stand out on shelf, is to design products that clearly fit in with the brand's visual identity" [E22]. The metric (PBCM) is the share of products using each asset, averaged across assets.
17. **Semiotic code states.** Raymond Williams (Marxism and Literature, 1977): "The residual, by definition, has been effectively formed in the past, but it is still active in the cultural process"; "By 'emergent' I mean, first, that new meanings and values, new practices, new relationships and kinds of relationship are continually being created" [E23]. Malcolm Evans introduced the "Residual, Dominant and Emergent mapping tool for cultural and communication codes" to marketing semiotics at Semiotic Solutions [E24]. Rachel Lawes, "Demystifying semiotics" (International Journal of Market Research 44(3), 2002) is the standard method paper [E25]; its full text is paywalled (WARC) and was not read, so no Lawes wording is quoted.
18. **Brands as behavior.** Brian Collins (COLLINS, 2016): today's brand problems are "more behavior-based than communications-based" [E26]. "Central organizing idea" has no single reachable primary source; the skill uses "brand behavior" and marks the method as opinion.

## Conflicting views
- Hue psychology claims versus evidence: see design-color psychology-brand (folklore list).
- Liking versus recognition as the test of a new identity [E2]: practice still runs preference focus groups; the evidence favors measuring fame and uniqueness.
